How scoring works

AI support agents don't read your article the way a person does. Before an agent ever sees your writing, a retrieval pipeline splits the article into pieces — usually one piece per section — and stores each piece separately. (Engineers call these pieces chunks; everywhere else, this tool just says sections.) When someone asks the agent a question, the pipeline fetches the few sections that best match the question, and the agent answers from those sections alone — without the title, without the intro, without whatever came before.

The traditional way to test for this is manual: pull a paragraph out of the article, read it cold with no surrounding context, rewrite it if it doesn't stand on its own — and repeat for every paragraph. This tool automates that test with deterministic, explainable checks. Same article in, same score out, every time. No AI models are involved in the scoring, and nothing you paste leaves your browser.

The score

Each of the five checks starts at 100 points. Every major finding subtracts 25, every minor subtracts 10, and info findings subtract nothing — they're worth knowing, not worth punishing. Only the first three findings per rule count toward the deduction; anything past that is listed but tagged "not double-counted — same habit", because ten instances of one habit is one lesson, not ten. A check never goes below 0.

The overall score is a weighted average of the five checks:

Self-contained sections25%

85 and up: Agent-ready. 60–84: Needs edits before your agent can rely on it. Below 60: An agent will struggle to answer from this. The same bands apply to each individual check (pass / needs work / fail).

Check 1 — Self-contained sections (25%)

AI agents retrieve individual sections, not whole articles. A section that only makes sense after reading what came before it will be retrieved alone — and misread alone. The retrieval principle: chunk self-containment.

  • Orphan opener (major, −25). The section's first sentence starts with a bare pronoun ("It", "This", "They…") or a connective ("However", "Therefore", "Additionally"…). Retrieved alone, the section opens mid-thought: the agent has no idea what "this" refers to, and a connective promises a contrast with text the agent never saw.
  • Cross-section pointer (major, −25). Phrases like "as mentioned above", "see below", or "in the previous section". The agent sees one section at a time — "above" doesn't exist for it.
  • Generic heading (minor, −10). Headings like "Overview", "Notes", "More information", or a bare "Step 3". The heading travels with its section as the label retrieval matches against; a generic label matches nothing in particular.
  • Orphan acronym (minor, −10). An acronym is spelled out once — "Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)" — and then used bare in a different section. The section holding the expansion may not be retrieved with the one using the acronym.

Check 2 — Answer first / BLUF (20%)

Agents (and the rankers that pick which sections to retrieve) weight the start of a section heavily. Burying the answer under context makes a section both harder to retrieve and harder to answer from. The principle: bottom line up front.

  • Preamble opener (major, −25). Throat-clearing first sentences: "In this article…", "Before you begin…", "It is important to note…". The first sentence is prime real estate — spend it on the answer, not on announcing the article.
  • Buried steps (minor, −10). Three or more sentences of prose before the section's steps. An agent asked "how do I…" should hit the steps immediately.
  • Slow start (minor, −10). A first paragraph over 60 words that neither gives an instruction nor restates the topic named in the heading. If the opening doesn't restate the topic, retrieval has weaker signal that this section answers the question.

Check 3 — Unresolved references (20%)

Pronouns and pointers that depend on distant context break when text is split into sections. The agent can't resolve them and may answer about the wrong thing. The principle: entity resolution — every "it" needs its noun nearby.

  • Dangling pointer (major, −25). "The following", "the above", or "the previous" with no list or table in the same section for it to point at. (A "the following" sentence that introduces the list right under it is fine — the target travels with the section.)
  • Bare demonstrative (minor, −10). A paragraph that opens "This causes…" or "Those require…". Each paragraph may be quoted alone in an answer; an opener like "This…" forces the agent to guess the referent. ("This setting controls…" is fine — the noun is right there.)
  • Ambiguous "it" (minor, −10). A paragraph that opens "It takes…" or "It fails…". Formal patterns like "It is recommended…" are not flagged.
  • Relative time (info, −0). "Currently", "recently", "soon", "the latest release". Agents can't tell when the article was written — "currently" becomes wrong silently. No points deducted; worth a date or a version number instead.

Check 4 — One idea per section (15%)

Retrieval matches a question to a section. A section covering three ideas matches all three questions weakly instead of one strongly. The principle: one idea per chunk.

  • Section too long (minor −10 over 300 words; major −25 over 500). Ingestion pipelines split oversized sections arbitrarily — mid-sentence, mid-idea — instead of where you would split them.
  • Topic shift (minor, −10). Two or more paragraphs in one section starting with "Additionally…", "You can also…", "Alternatively…". Each extra idea dilutes the section's match to any one question.
  • No headings (major, −25). A document over 200 words with no headings at all. Without headings, the splitting is done by a token counter instead of by you.

Check 5 — Structure signals (20%)

Headings, lists, and tables are structure an agent can parse reliably; walls of prose are not. The principle: structure parseability.

  • Low heading density (minor, −10). More than 400 words per heading on average. Fewer headings means bigger retrieval units and vaguer matches.
  • Heading jump (info, −0). A heading level skipping down more than one step (# straight to ###). Skipped levels scramble the section tree some ingestion pipelines rely on.
  • Comparison in prose (minor, −10). Two or more "whereas / compared to / in contrast" sentences and no table in the section. Agents answer comparison questions far more reliably from a table.
  • Sequence in prose (minor, −10). "First…, then…, next…, finally…" packed into one paragraph with no numbered list in the section. Steps hidden in a paragraph get paraphrased and reordered; a numbered list gets quoted.
  • Wall of text (minor, −10). Any single paragraph over 120 words. Long paragraphs are split blindly mid-thought by ingestion pipelines.

Strengths

The report also celebrates what's already agent-ready — no points, just recognition: question- or task-form headings ("How do I…", "Reset your token"), tables for comparisons, and numbered steps. If your article shows strengths, keep writing like that.

Honest limitations

Every rule here is a deterministic heuristic tuned for English-language KB writing — no machine learning, no API calls. Heuristics have edge cases; when a finding is wrong for your article, you'll be able to see exactly why it fired, judge it yourself, and ignore it with a clear conscience. A score that can't explain itself is a toy; every deduction on this page can.

One consequence of the weighting is worth knowing: the checks about meaning — self-containment, answer-first, references — carry 65% of the score, and pure structure carries 35%. An article whose only problem is structural (one giant unbroken section, say) can still land in the 80s. Read the five check scores, not just the headline number: a failing check is a failing check, whatever the total says.
