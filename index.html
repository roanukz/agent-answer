Will My Agent Answer This? — Product Teardown

Support and knowledge teams are being told to make their knowledge bases "AI-ready." The only test on offer is manual: pull a paragraph out, strip its context, read it cold, rewrite it if it fails — for every paragraph of every article. This is the product thinking behind a free browser tool that automates that test: the job, the evidence, the eleven tools I killed to find this one, the eight decisions I committed to, and what I would kill next.

The short version

Summary

  • The problem is real and documented. Vendors literally prescribe a manual, per-paragraph test for AI-readiness — the quotes are in Part B, and they describe the same chore in almost the same words. Meanwhile Gartner projects that by 2029, agentic AI will autonomously resolve 80% of common customer service issues without human intervention. Every one of those agents answers from a knowledge base that was written for humans.
  • The gap was found by elimination, not inspiration. Roughly fourteen candidate tool spaces were researched for saturation before anything was built; eleven were killed. This was the only space where every adjacent category misses the same combination: framed for the knowledge-base author, scores self-containment, takes pasted text, and runs entirely in the browser.
  • Capability is not the constraint — trust and distribution are. That is why the score is a deterministic, explainable rubric rather than a black-box model call, why the architecture is 100% client-side, and why the top three items on the RICE roadmap are distribution and validation rather than features.

Part A

The problem

Support and knowledge teams are being told that their knowledge bases must be rewritten to be "AI-ready" so a retrieval agent can answer from them. The mechanic behind that instruction is the whole problem: an AI support agent does not read your article. A pipeline splits it into pieces — usually one per section — stores each separately, then fetches the few that best match a question and answers from those alone. No title, no introduction, none of the paragraphs that came before.

So a section that only makes sense in sequence is a section the agent will get wrong, and the only test available today is manual: pull a paragraph out, remove its context, read it cold, rewrite it if it fails, and repeat — for every paragraph of every article. Two vendors describe that exact chore in Part B, in almost the same words. Nobody does it at the scale of a real knowledge base, because at that scale it is not a test, it is a second job.

Jobs To Be Done — describe the progress the user is trying to make, not the feature they asked for — puts the job like this:

The job

"When my team turns on an AI agent over our knowledge base, I want to know which articles the agent cannot answer from — before customers find out — so that the agent deflects tickets instead of generating hallucination complaints."

Note whose job that is. Not the engineer configuring the pipeline, and not the marketer chasing citations in AI search. It belongs to the person who owns the content, whose deflection target is now attached to somebody else's model, and whose failure mode is a customer being told something untrue with total confidence.

The forces of progress

The Forces of Progress model asks what pushes someone toward a new solution, what pulls them to it, what makes them anxious, and what holds them to the current habit. Adoption happens only when the first two beat the second two.

Forces acting on a knowledge manager deciding whether to run their articles through this tool.
Force What it looks like here
Push of the situation Agent deployments are mandated top-down and arrive with deflection targets attached — and most existing content will not pass the standalone test. Rezolve.ai's own guidance is that only a small percentage of content is typically AI-ready as written (Part B).
Pull of the solution The manual per-paragraph chore, automated. One paste, every section tested in seconds, the failing passages highlighted in place, and a prioritised list of what to fix first.
Anxiety of the new Two distinct anxieties, not one. First: pasting confidential internal knowledge-base content into an unknown web tool. Second: getting back a score too opaque to defend to stakeholders — "the website said 62" is not an argument you can win a content-freeze meeting with.
Habit of the present Editorial style guides and manual review, plus the sturdiest habit of all: "we'll fix articles when the agent fails on them."

The two anxieties are the part worth dwelling on, because neither is a marketing problem: each one dictated an architecture decision. Confidentiality is why the tool is 100% client-side with no runtime network activity at all, and why that promise is verifiable by the user rather than asserted by me. Defensibility is why the score is a deterministic rubric that shows its work rather than a model's opinion. Part E returns to both, as decisions 1 and 2.

The load-bearing observation: the two reasons a knowledge manager would refuse this tool are the two things it was architected around.

Part B

The evidence

I am not inferring this problem from first principles. The manual test is being prescribed in public, by the vendors selling the agents.

  • SearchUnify — 2026 best-practices guide

    "Pull a paragraph from the middle of each one, remove the title and surrounding context, and read it the way an agent would, as a standalone fragment… if a section can't be understood without reading what came before it, it needs to be rewritten."

  • Rankscale — describing the identical manual workflow

    "Cut it, paste it into a blank document. Read it cold… Rewrite. Repeat for every paragraph."

  • Rezolve.ai — on the state of existing content

    "it's common for only a small percentage of content to be AI-ready in its current form."

  • RefineTrain AI — Y Combinator, W22
    A company that exists solely to rewrite documentation for LLM retrieval. Venture funding has been raised against this exact pain, which tells me it is a fundable problem and not a niche irritation.
  • Gartner — press release, March 2025
    The macro driver: by 2029, agentic AI will autonomously resolve 80% of common customer service issues without human intervention, leading to a 30% reduction in operational costs. Gartner press release.

Read together: the first three establish that the test exists and is manual, the fourth that someone is being paid to do it by hand, the fifth the volume of agent deployments that will need it.

Freshness check, August 2026

I re-verified the gap immediately before building, because a gap this obvious invites arrivals. The nearest new arrival is Rank Prompt's free AI Readiness Checker — a generative-search tool aimed at marketers and at URLs, not a paste-an-article linter for knowledge-base authors. Different user, different input, different question. The gap holds.

Where this evidence is weak, stated up front

The demand evidence skews toward vendor and practitioner blogs plus one Y Combinator company; three of the five sources above are vendor guides, cited by publisher and title. What I do not have is raw first-person complaint from working technical writers — the "I spent my Friday doing this by hand" post. A targeted community-evidence pass is item three on the roadmap, deliberately scheduled before launch messaging is finalised: messaging written ahead of that evidence would be my hypothesis wearing a customer's voice.

Part C

Market and incentives

Two numbers, both external, and no others. Demand: Gartner's projection above — 80% of common customer service issues resolved autonomously by 2029. Market size: the knowledge management software market is projected to reach $75.22 billion by 2034, growing at a 12.3% CAGR (Straits Research). I am not claiming a share of that number. I am using it to locate the tool inside it.

The tool sits at the author workflow layer of that market — where an article either becomes agent-answerable or does not, upstream of both the pipeline and the agent. It is also the layer enterprise platforms monetise as paid readiness audits and consulting engagements rather than as self-serve software.

Why incumbents will not build this

What follows is incentive analysis, and I am interpreting motives from business models rather than reporting stated intent. I mark it as inference, not fact.

  • Enterprise knowledge management platforms (Bloomfire, SearchUnify, Rezolve, DevRev) sell AI-readiness as audits, premium modules and services. A free, self-serve, paste-text linter cannibalises exactly that line item. They could build this in a sprint; they are structurally disinclined to ship it for nothing.
  • Generative-search tools — GEO and AEO, meaning optimisation for citation inside AI answers rather than for search rankings — monetise marketers chasing visibility. Their economics point at public web pages and SEO budgets. An internal knowledge-base author has neither a public URL nor a budget line.
  • Retrieval tooling vendors target the developers configuring pipelines, not the writers producing the content those pipelines ingest. Their users can already inspect how a document was split; that is a debugging view, not an editorial verdict.

So the wedge is not a technology gap — any of those three could build this. It is an incentive gap, which is a more durable thing to build behind, and the same shape as the argument in my previous teardown about why Google will not fix Takeout's metadata: a gap that exists because nobody thought of it closes the moment somebody does; a gap that exists because closing it would cost the incumbent revenue stays open.

The chunk visualisers are the closest miss and the most instructive one. They split a document and preview the pieces — genuinely the right raw material — but they stop before the judgement. Showing an author their article in fragments answers "what will the pipeline store?" It does not answer "can an agent answer from this?" The GEO and AEO checkers do score structure and answer-first writing, which overlaps — but they score a public URL for citation visibility, on a server, for a marketer. Part E

What I built

A single static page. You paste a knowledge-base article, and it returns a 0–100 agent-answerability score across five checks — self-contained sections, answer first, unresolved references, one idea per section, structure signals — weighted 25/20/20/15/20, with 19 deterministic rules underneath. Every finding is highlighted in place in your article, and every deduction is itemised. Nothing is uploaded, because there is nothing to upload to.

I use Marty Cagan's four product risks — value (will anyone want it), usability (can they use it), feasibility (can it be built), viability (does it work for the business) — as the spine, because each decision below exists to retire a specific one, and naming which one keeps the reasoning honest.

Decision 1

A deterministic, explainable rubric instead of an LLM grader

Retires: value · viability of trust

The obvious build is to send the article to a model and ask for a score. I did not. Every deduction here shows the offending text, what is wrong with it, why it matters to a retrieval agent, and what to do instead. A score that cannot explain itself is a toy, and a black box is exactly what the professional audience this targets would reject: the knowledge manager justifying a content freeze needs to show a colleague the sentence and the reason, not a number from a model that may score differently tomorrow.

  • CostCruder than semantic analysis. It will produce false positives on some legitimate writing styles.
  • BoughtReproducibility — same article in, same score out, every time — defensibility in front of stakeholders, and zero servers.
Decision 2

100% client-side, zero runtime network activity

Retires: viability · the confidentiality anxiety

Knowledge-base articles are confidential by default: internal procedures, unreleased features, escalation paths, customer names in examples. Any tool that asks an author to paste that in has to answer "where does this go?" before it earns the first paste. Here it goes nowhere, and the promise is verifiable rather than asserted: turn off Wi-Fi and analyse again. The bundle makes no network calls at all, which you can check in the browser's network panel.

  • CostNo telemetry, and therefore no usage data — not now, not later.
  • BoughtThe paste-without-asking-permission adoption path. An author can try this on a real article at their desk without opening a vendor security review.
Decision 3

Rich-paste capture, converting HTML to markdown

Retires: usability

Authors do not copy markdown. They copy from a rendered page in Zendesk, Confluence or ServiceNow, and a plain-text paste of that arrives with the headings flattened into ordinary lines. That matters more here than in most tools: headings are where sections begin, the section is the unit every check scores, and four of the five checks have rules that read the headings themselves. So the tool intercepts the HTML flavour of the clipboard and converts it — the analysis is accurate on the input people actually have, not the input the format documentation imagines.

  • CostA conversion dependency and its edge cases, in a project that otherwise has almost none.
  • BoughtThe first paste works. There is no second chance at a first paste.
Decision 4

Per-rule deduction caps

Retires: value

Only the first three findings per rule count against the score. Further instances are still listed, tagged as the same habit rather than as new evidence. One bad habit repeated thirty times should read as one lesson, not a zero — a rubric that returns a zero teaches the author nothing except that the tool is hostile. This is the change that most affects whether a score gets shared with a team or quietly closed.

  • CostScore compression at the low end: two genuinely bad articles can land closer together than they deserve.
  • BoughtScores that feel fair — and fairness is what makes a rubric adoptable.
Decision 5

Author language, not developer language

Retires: usability

The interface says "section," never "chunk." The retrieval concepts the rubric depends on — self-containment, entity resolution, why the start of a passage is weighted heavily — are taught in the explainer rather than assumed as vocabulary. This sounds cosmetic. It is the positioning made concrete: the category is full of tools that speak to people who already know what a chunk is, and empty of tools that speak to the person writing the content.

  • CostLess resonance with the developer crowd, who are also the crowd most likely to upvote a launch post.
  • BoughtThe audience that is actually underserved.
Decision 6

Positive findings, not only deductions

Retires: value · a Kano delighter

The report calls out what is already working: question-form headings, tables used for comparisons, numbered steps for procedures. None of it earns points — this is recognition, not scoring. A linter that only subtracts trains the author to avoid it, and an author who runs this once has not changed how they write. An author who runs it twice has.

  • CostScreen space, and some of the report's attention budget.
  • BoughtThe tool reads as a coach rather than a critic — which is what changes whether it gets opened a second time.
Decision 7

A unit-tested rule engine with near-miss tests

Retires: feasibility

Every rule ships with two tests: a case that must trigger it, and a near-miss that must not. "This setting controls the retry interval" is not a dangling pronoun, even though it opens with "This," and a rule that cannot tell the difference will flag competent writing and lose the author's trust in one sitting. The near-miss test is the one that matters, because the failure mode of a heuristic linter is not missing a problem, it is crying wolf.

  • CostSlower rule authoring — two tests before a rule counts as done.
  • BoughtRubric integrity. In this product, the rubric is the product; the interface is just how you read it.
Decision 8

A written kill list

Retires: viability · focus

No accounts, no server, no platform integrations, no batch mode, no auto-rewrite, no file upload — in v1, on purpose, written down before the first line of code so that later enthusiasm could not relitigate it. Every one of those features is defensible in isolation. Together they are a different product with a server, a privacy caveat and no ship date.

  • CostEvery power user will ask for one of them, and some will leave.
  • BoughtA tool that shipped, and a privacy promise with no asterisks.

Kano classification

The Kano model sorts features by how satisfaction responds to them: must-bes are unnoticed when present and fatal when absent, performance attributes scale with quality, delighters are unrequested and disproportionately memorable. Accurate heading parsing is a must-be — get it wrong and every section boundary is wrong, so every check is wrong, and nobody reports it as a parsing bug because they simply stop believing the score. Inline highlights and per-check scores are performance attributes: more precise is linearly better. The delighters are the two things nobody asked for — the "why this matters to an agent" line on every deduction, and a privacy claim you can verify by turning off the network.

Business viability, stated plainly

There is no revenue model and none is planned. Willingness to pay clearly exists in this market — enterprise platforms charge for exactly this analysis, packaged as audits and consulting — but free-with-no-strings is the positioning here, not a monetisation deferred to a later slide. The return I am underwriting is demonstrated product judgement: this is the public version of the work I do, which is structuring knowledge content so autonomous agents can resolve tickets. I am not going to manufacture a business case for a portfolio artifact.

What I got wrong, and what is still open

  1. The weights are judgement, not evidence. 25/20/20/15/20 across the five checks is my ranking of what breaks retrieval worst. There is no dataset of "articles agents failed on" behind it. The weights are defensible; they are not validated.
  2. Deterministic heuristics will misfire on legitimate styles. The per-rule caps and the near-miss tests bound the damage, but a calibration pass against real production knowledge-base articles is required before the score should be promoted as authoritative rather than indicative.
  3. The demand evidence is vendor-sourced. It is strong on the existence of the manual test and thin on the first-person experience of doing it. The community-evidence pass is item three on the roadmap, ahead of launch messaging rather than after it.
  4. The heuristics are English-only and quietly weaker on everything else. The tool warns when the text does not look like English, and a warning is not a fix.
  5. Zero telemetry means the rubric can never be tuned from usage. No funnel, no drop-off data, no distribution of real scores to calibrate the bands against. That is the price of the privacy promise, and I am paying it knowingly and permanently — the alternative would falsify the one claim the tool is built on.

Part F

Part G

What I would kill

The Blue Ocean ERRC grid asks what to eliminate, reduce, raise and create relative to the category's norms. It maps unusually well onto a kill list, because the strategy here is to compete on axes the incumbents ignore rather than to out-feature them on the axes they own.

Eliminate

  • Servers and uploads.
  • Accounts.
  • Telemetry.
  • Platform integrations.
  • Generative auto-rewrite.

Kill on sight. Each one breaks either the privacy promise or the focus, and two of them break both.

Reduce

  • Scope: one article at a time, pasted in. No queues, no crawling.
  • Configuration: zero settings. The rubric is the opinion, and a rubric you can tune is a rubric you can talk out of a bad score.

Raise

  • Explainability: every point lost is accounted for, in the author's own text.
  • Trust: a privacy claim you can verify offline.
  • Accessibility of the underlying retrieval concepts to people who do not build pipelines.

Create

  • A free, self-serve, zero-install category for what the enterprise layer sells as consulting: the manual per-paragraph AI-readiness test, automated.

Appendix

Method made visible

The frameworks that did real work here, and what each one was load-bearing for. None of them is decoration; where one did not change a decision, I have left it out and said so below.

Framework What it did here
Jobs To Be Done Defined the job and identified whose job it is — the content owner, not the pipeline engineer or the marketer.
Forces of Progress Mapped the two adoption anxieties directly onto two architecture decisions. The essay's load-bearing link.

Deliberately left out

No personas: there is a single, well-understood user here — my own professional peer group — and inventing three named archetypes of one person I already understand would be ceremony, not insight. No North Star metric and no HEART: both require telemetry, eliminated on principle in decision 2, and a metric I cannot measure invites false confidence. No OKRs: this is a portfolio artifact, not an organisation, and there is no one to align.

Naming what I left out, and why, is itself a position.
