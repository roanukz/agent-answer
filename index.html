Product teardown

Will My Agent Answer This? — Product Teardown

Support and knowledge teams are being told to get their knowledge bases ready for AI agents, and the check they are handed is a manual one: take a single paragraph, cover up everything around it, read it cold, and rewrite it if it no longer makes sense on its own. Repeating that for every paragraph of every article is work no team ever gets to the end of, and it asks a writer to guess how a machine will read their words. So I built a tool that runs the same check on every section in seconds and applies the same judgement every time. Below is the product thinking behind it: the job, the evidence, the eleven ideas I cut to find this one, the eight decisions I committed to, and what I would cut next.

The short version

Summary

  • The problem is real and documented. Vendors are publishing step-by-step instructions for testing AI readiness by hand, and two of them describe the same chore in almost the same words (Part B). Meanwhile Gartner projects that by 2029, agentic AI will autonomously resolve 80% of common customer service issues without human intervention, and every one of those agents will be answering from a knowledge base that was written for people.
  • The gap was found by elimination, not inspiration. Around fourteen candidate ideas were researched before anything was built, and eleven of them were cut because good free tools already existed. This was the only space where every nearby product misses the same combination: written for the knowledge-base author, scores whether a section stands on its own, takes pasted text, and runs entirely in the browser.
  • Capability is not the constraint, but trust and distribution are. That is why the score comes from a published set of fixed rules rather than a model's opinion, why nothing you paste ever leaves your browser, and why the top three items on the roadmap are about reaching people and testing my own evidence rather than about adding features.

Part A

The problem

Support and knowledge teams are being told their articles must be rewritten so that an AI agent can answer from them. The reason is worth spelling out, because it is the whole problem. An AI support agent does not read your article: software splits it into pieces, usually one piece per section, and files each piece separately. When a customer asks a question, the software pulls out the two or three pieces that look like the closest match, and the agent answers from those alone, without ever seeing the title, the introduction, or any of the paragraphs that came before.

A section that only makes sense in sequence is therefore a section the agent will get wrong, and the test for that is currently a manual one. You lift a paragraph out, strip away its context, read it cold, rewrite it if it fails, and then start again on the next paragraph. Two vendors describe exactly that routine in Part B, in almost the same words. It fails for two reasons: it asks a writer to imagine how retrieval software will read their sentences, which is not a skill anyone was hired for, and at the size of a real knowledge base it stops being a test and becomes a second job that never gets finished.

That is the gap a purpose-built checker fills. Knowing what breaks an answer belongs in a tool that applies the same judgement to every section, rather than in the head of each author on each paragraph.

Jobs To Be Done is a simple discipline that describes the progress someone is trying to make rather than the feature they asked for. Here the job is:

The job

"When my team turns on an AI agent over our knowledge base, I want to know which articles the agent cannot answer from, before customers find out, so that the agent deflects tickets instead of generating hallucination complaints."

It is worth noticing whose job that is. It does not belong to the engineer who set up the retrieval software, and it does not belong to the marketer chasing mentions in AI search results. It belongs to the person who owns the content, whose target for tickets resolved without a human is now tied to somebody else's model, and whose worst day is a customer being told something untrue with complete confidence.

The forces of progress

The Forces of Progress model asks four questions about anyone weighing up a change: what is pushing them away from how they work now, what is pulling them toward something new, what makes them nervous, and what keeps them where they are. A product is adopted only when the first two outweigh the last two.

Most existing content will not pass the standalone test, and Rezolve.ai's own guidance says that only a small percentage of content is usually AI-ready as written (Part B).
Pull of the solution The same test, done for you. One paste checks every section in seconds, marks the weak passages where they sit, and ranks what to fix first.
Anxiety of the new There are two separate worries here rather than one. The first is pasting confidential internal content into a website nobody has vetted. Those two worries deserve the attention, because neither of them is a marketing problem and each one settled a piece of the architecture. Confidentiality is why the whole tool runs inside your browser and makes no network requests at all, and why you can check that for yourself instead of taking my word for it. Defensibility is why the score comes from fixed rules that show their working rather than from a model's opinion. Part E comes back to both of them, as decisions 1 and 2.

The load-bearing observation: the two reasons a knowledge manager would refuse this tool are the two things it was architected around.

Part B

The evidence

I did not reason this problem out from first principles. The manual test is being recommended in public, by the vendors selling the agents.

  • SearchUnify 2026 best-practices guide

    "Pull a paragraph from the middle of each one, remove the title and surrounding context, and read it the way an agent would, as a standalone fragment… if a section can't be understood without reading what came before it, it needs to be rewritten."

  • Rankscale describing the identical manual workflow

    "Cut it, paste it into a blank document. Read it cold… Rewrite. Repeat for every paragraph."

  • Rezolve.ai on the state of existing content

    "it's common for only a small percentage of content to be AI-ready in its current form."

  • RefineTrain AI Y Combinator, W22
    A company whose entire business is rewriting documentation so that AI retrieval can use it. Venture money has been raised against this exact problem, which tells me it is a fundable one rather than a private irritation.
  • Gartner press release, March 2025
    The wider driver: by 2029, agentic AI will autonomously resolve 80% of common customer service issues without human intervention, leading to a 30% reduction in operational costs. Gartner press release.

Read together, the first three sources show that the test exists and is done by hand, the fourth shows that someone is being paid to do it, and the fifth shows how many agent rollouts are going to need it.

Freshness check, August 2026

I checked the gap again immediately before building, because a gap this obvious invites company. The closest new arrival is Rank Prompt's free AI Readiness Checker, which is built for marketers and checks a public web address for visibility in AI search results. It serves a different user, takes a different input and answers a different question, so the gap holds.

Where this evidence is weak, stated up front

The demand evidence leans on vendor and practitioner blogs plus one Y Combinator company, and three of the five sources above are vendor guides, cited by publisher and title. What I do not have is first-hand complaint from working technical writers, the "I spent my Friday doing this by hand" post. A focused pass through those communities is item three on the roadmap, and it is deliberately scheduled before any launch messaging is finalised, because messaging written ahead of that evidence would only be my own hypothesis in a customer's voice.

Part C

Market and incentives

There are two outside numbers on this page and no others. On demand, there is Gartner's projection above, that 80% of common customer service issues will be resolved without a human by 2029. On market size, knowledge management software is projected to reach $75.22 billion by 2034, growing at 12.3% a year (Straits Research). I am not claiming a share of that number, only using it to show where this tool sits inside the market.

It sits in the author's workflow, at the point where an article either becomes answerable by an agent or does not. That point is upstream of the retrieval software and upstream of the agent itself, and it is also the point that enterprise platforms sell as a paid readiness audit or a consulting engagement rather than as software you can use yourself.

Why the incumbents will not build this

What follows reads business models and works backwards to motives, so it is my interpretation rather than anything these companies have said, and I flag it as such.

  • Enterprise knowledge platforms such as Bloomfire, SearchUnify, Rezolve and DevRev sell AI readiness as audits, premium modules and services, and a free tool you can paste an article into eats into exactly that revenue. They could build it in a sprint, but they have no reason to give it away.
  • Generative search tools, sold as GEO or AEO, help marketers get quoted inside AI answers rather than ranked in search results. Their money comes from public web pages and marketing budgets, whereas an internal knowledge-base author has neither a public page nor a budget line.
  • Companies selling retrieval infrastructure aim at the developers who configure it rather than the writers who produce what it reads. Those developers can already see how a document was divided up, which gives them a debugging view rather than an editorial verdict.

So this is not a gap in technology, because any of those three could build it tomorrow. It is a gap in incentives, which is a sturdier thing to sit behind, and it is the same shape as the argument in my previous teardown about why Google will not fix the metadata in Takeout exports. A gap that exists because nobody thought of it closes the moment somebody does, whereas a gap that exists because closing it would cost the incumbent money stays open.

The retrieval preview tools are the closest miss and the most instructive one. They divide a document up and show you the pieces, which is the right raw material, but they stop before the judgement. Showing an author their article in fragments answers the question "what will the software store?" without answering "can an agent answer from this?", and the second question is the one the job asks. The AI search checkers do score structure and answer-first writing, which overlaps, but they score a public web page, for visibility in AI answers, on a server, for a marketer, and every part of that sentence describes a different product.

Part E

What I built

The tool is a single web page. You paste in a knowledge-base article and get back a score out of 100 for how well an agent could answer from it, across five checks: whether sections stand on their own, whether the answer comes first, whether references point at something nearby, whether each section sticks to one idea, and whether the structure is clear. The checks are weighted 25/20/20/15/20 and there are 19 rules underneath them. Every finding is marked in your own text and every point lost is itemised, and nothing is uploaded anywhere, because there is nowhere to upload it to.

Marty Cagan's four product risks are the spine of this section: value (will anyone want it), usability (can they use it), feasibility (can it be built) and viability (does it work for the business). Each decision below exists to settle one of them, and naming which one keeps the reasoning honest.

Decision 1

Fixed, explainable rules instead of an AI grader

Risk addressed: value, and trust

The obvious build is to send the article to a language model and ask it for a score, and I chose not to. Every point lost here shows the sentence that caused it, what is wrong with it, why it matters to an agent, and what to write instead. A score that cannot explain itself is a toy, and a score out of a black box is exactly what this audience would reject, because a knowledge manager asking to pause publishing for a rewrite has to show a colleague the sentence and the reason rather than a number from a model that might say something different tomorrow.

  • CostBlunter than judging meaning properly, and it will flag some perfectly good writing.
  • BoughtThe same article always gets the same score, the result can be defended in a meeting, and there are no servers to run.
Decision 2

Everything runs in your browser, with no network requests

Risk addressed: viability, and the confidentiality worry

Knowledge-base articles are confidential by default, because they hold internal procedures, unreleased features, escalation paths and customer names in examples. Any tool that asks an author to paste that in has to answer "where does this go?" before it earns the first paste. Here the answer is that it goes nowhere, and you do not have to believe me, because you can turn off your Wi-Fi and run it again. The page makes no network requests at all, and you can confirm that in your own browser.

  • CostNo usage data, now or ever.
  • BoughtAn author can try this on a real article without asking anyone's permission first.
Decision 3

Keeping the formatting when you paste

Risk addressed: usability

Authors do not write in plain text markup. They copy from a finished page in Zendesk, Confluence or ServiceNow, and a plain-text paste of that arrives with the headings flattened into ordinary sentences. That matters more here than it would in most tools, because headings are where sections begin, the section is the unit every check scores, and four of the five checks have rules that read the headings themselves. So the tool takes the formatted version of what you copied and converts it, which means the analysis is accurate on the input people really have instead of the tidy input a file format imagines.

  • CostOne conversion library and its edge cases, in a project that otherwise has almost none.
  • BoughtThe first paste works, and there is no second chance at a first paste.
Decision 4

A cap on how often one rule can cost you points

Risk addressed: value

Only the first three findings from any single rule count against the score, and the rest are still listed but marked as the same habit rather than as fresh evidence. One bad habit repeated thirty times should read as one lesson rather than as a zero, because a rubric that hands back a zero teaches the author nothing except that the tool is hostile. Of everything here, this is the change that most affects whether a score gets shared with the team or quietly closed.

  • CostTwo genuinely poor articles can end up scoring closer together than they deserve.
  • BoughtScores that feel fair, and fairness is what makes a rubric worth adopting.
Decision 5

The words authors use, not the words engineers use

Risk addressed: usability

The interface says "section" and never "chunk", and the ideas the rubric rests on, such as whether a passage stands on its own and whether a pronoun has anything nearby to refer to, are explained rather than assumed. This sounds cosmetic, but it is the positioning made concrete: the category is full of tools that speak to people who already know the jargon and empty of tools that speak to the person writing the words.

  • CostLess appeal to the developer crowd, who are also the crowd most likely to upvote a launch post.
  • BoughtThe audience nobody else is serving.
Decision 6

Saying what already works, not just what is broken

Risk addressed: value, as a Kano delighter

The report also picks out what is already working, such as headings written as questions, tables used for comparisons and numbered lists for procedures. None of it earns points, because it is recognition rather than scoring. A tool that only ever subtracts teaches authors to avoid it, and an author who runs this once has not changed how they write, whereas an author who runs it twice has.

  • CostScreen space, and part of the report's attention budget.
  • BoughtIt reads as a coach rather than a critic, and that is what decides whether it gets opened a second time.
Decision 7

Every rule tested twice, including a near miss

Risk addressed: feasibility

Every rule ships with two tests: an example it must catch, and a near miss it must leave alone. "This setting controls the retry interval" is not a vague pronoun even though it starts with "This", and a rule that cannot tell those two apart will flag good writing and lose the author's trust in one sitting. The near miss is the test that matters, because a rule-based checker rarely fails by missing problems and usually fails by crying wolf.

  • CostSlower rule writing, since a rule is not finished until both tests exist.
  • BoughtA rubric that holds up. In this product the rubric is the product, and the interface is only how you read it.
Decision 8

A cut list, written down before the build

Risk addressed: viability, and focus

Version 1 has no accounts, no server, no integrations with other platforms, no batch mode, no automatic rewriting and no file upload, and all of that was written down before the first line of code so that later enthusiasm could not reopen the argument. Every one of those features is defensible on its own, but together they add up to a different product with a server, a privacy caveat and no shipping date.

  • CostEvery power user will ask for one of them, and some will leave.
  • BoughtA tool that shipped, and a privacy promise with no small print.

Kano classification

The Kano model sorts features by how people react to them. Must-haves go unnoticed when they work and cause real anger when they fail, performance features get better the more you invest in them, and delighters are the things nobody asked for that stick in the memory. Reading headings correctly is the must-have here, because getting it wrong puts every section boundary in the wrong place, which makes every check wrong, and nobody will report that as a parsing bug because they will simply stop believing the score. Marking passages in place and scoring each check separately are performance features, where more precision is straightforwardly better. The two delighters are the line on every finding explaining why it matters to an agent, and a privacy claim you can test by switching off your network.

Business viability, stated plainly

There is no revenue model and none is planned. People clearly pay for this kind of analysis, since enterprise platforms sell it as audits and consulting, but free with no strings attached is the position here rather than a plan to charge later. What I am betting on is the judgement on display, in the public version of the work I do, which is organising knowledge content so that autonomous agents can resolve tickets. I am not going to invent a business case for a portfolio piece.

What I got wrong, and what is still open

  1. The weights are judgement, not evidence. The 25/20/20/15/20 split is my own ranking of what damages an answer most, and there is no dataset of articles that agents failed on behind it. The weights are defensible, but they are not validated.
  2. Fixed rules will misfire on good writing. The per-rule cap and the near-miss tests limit the damage, but the score is a strong hint rather than a verdict until it has been calibrated against real production articles.
  3. The demand evidence comes from vendors. It is strong on the existence of the manual test and thin on what it feels like to do it, which is why closing that gap is item three on the roadmap, scheduled before launch messaging rather than after it.
  4. The rules only work properly in English. The tool warns you when the text does not look like English, and a warning is not a fix.
  5. With no usage data, the rubric can never be tuned by watching people use it. There is no funnel, no drop-off and no spread of real scores to calibrate the bands against. That is the price of the privacy promise and I am paying it knowingly and permanently, because the alternative would break the one claim the tool rests on.

Part G

What I would cut

The Blue Ocean ERRC grid asks what to eliminate, what to reduce, what to raise and what to create, measured against the norms of the category. It suits a cut list, because the plan here is to compete on the things competitors ignore rather than to out-feature them on the things they own.

Eliminate

  • Servers and uploads.
  • Accounts.
  • Usage tracking.
  • Integrations with other platforms.
  • Automatic rewriting.

Each one of these breaks either the privacy promise or the focus, and two of them break both, so they go on sight.

Reduce

  • Scope, to one article at a time, pasted in. No queues and no crawling.
  • Settings, to none at all. The rubric is the opinion, and a rubric you can adjust is a rubric you can talk out of a bad score.

Raise

  • Explanation, so that every point lost is accounted for in the author's own words.
  • Trust, through a privacy claim you can test offline.
  • Plain language, so the ideas behind the score are open to people who do not build software.

Create

  • A free, self-serve, nothing-to-install version of what the enterprise layer sells as consulting: the manual paragraph-by-paragraph AI-readiness test, done automatically.

Appendix

Method made visible

These are the frameworks that did real work here, and what each one was load-bearing for. Where one did not change a decision, I left it out and said so below.

Frameworks used, and the work each one did.
Framework What it did here
Jobs To Be Done Defined the job, and identified whose job it is: the person who owns the content, rather than the engineer or the marketer.
Forces of Progress Mapped the two reasons someone would refuse the tool onto two architecture decisions, which is the load-bearing link in this essay.
Kano model Separated the must-have of reading headings correctly from the performance features of marked passages and per-check scores, and from the delighters of the "why this matters" lines and the offline proof.
Cagan's four risks Structured all eight decisions, and made each one name the risk it settles rather than the feature it adds.
RICE Put distribution above features, and pushed the most interesting engineering work into fourth place.
Blue Ocean ERRC Positioning, and the discipline of the cut list.

Deliberately left out

I did not write personas, because there is one well-understood user here, my own professional peers, and inventing three named archetypes of a person I already understand would be box-ticking rather than insight. I did not set a North Star metric or use the HEART framework, because both need usage data, which decision 2 rules out, and a metric I cannot measure only invites false confidence. I did not write OKRs, because this is a portfolio piece rather than an organisation, and there is nobody to align.

Naming what I left out, and why, is itself a position.
