Product teardown

Will My Agent Answer This?

Knowledge teams are being told to get their articles ready for AI agents, and the test they are handed is a manual one: cut out a paragraph, read it with no context around it, and rewrite it if it no longer stands up. Nobody gets to the end of that by hand, so I built a tool that runs it on every section in seconds. This is the thinking behind it.

The short version

Summary

  • The problem is documented, not inferred. Two vendors publish the same manual test in almost the same words (Part 2), and Gartner projects that by 2029, agentic AI will autonomously resolve 80% of common customer service issues without human intervention. Those agents will be answering from knowledge bases written for people.
  • The gap was found by elimination. Around fourteen ideas were researched for saturation before anything was built, and eleven were cut. This was the only space where no existing product combines all four of: written for the knowledge-base author, scores whether a section stands alone, takes pasted text, and runs entirely in the browser.
  • Trust and distribution are the constraints, not capability. Hence fixed published rules instead of a model's opinion, nothing leaving the browser, and a roadmap whose top three items are reach and evidence rather than features.

Part 1

The problem

The problem is that an AI support agent never reads your article. Software splits it into pieces, usually one per section, and files each piece separately. When a customer asks a question, it retrieves the few pieces that best match and the agent answers from those alone, without the title, the introduction, or the paragraphs that came before. So a section that only makes sense in sequence is a section the agent will get wrong.

The test for that is manual, and it is the routine two vendors describe in Part 2. It fails for two reasons. It asks a writer to imagine how retrieval software reads, which is not a skill anyone was hired for, and at the size of a real knowledge base it is a second job rather than a test. Knowing what breaks an answer belongs in a tool that applies the same judgement to every section, not in the head of each author on each paragraph.

Jobs To Be Done describes the progress someone wants rather than the feature they asked for. Here the job is:

The job

"When my team turns on an AI agent over our knowledge base, I want to know which articles the agent cannot answer from, before customers find out, so that the agent deflects tickets instead of generating hallucination complaints."

That job belongs to the person who owns the content, not to the engineer who configured the retrieval software or the marketer chasing mentions in AI search. Their target for tickets resolved without a human is now tied to somebody else's model, and their worst day is a customer being told something untrue with complete confidence.

The forces of progress

The Forces of Progress model asks what pushes someone away from how they work now, what pulls them to something new, what makes them nervous, and what keeps them where they are. Adoption needs the first two to outweigh the last two.

Forces acting on a knowledge manager deciding whether to run their articles through this tool.
Force What it looks like here
Push of the situation Agent rollouts are decided high up and arrive with a deflection target attached. Rezolve.ai's own guidance says only a small percentage of content is usually AI-ready as written (Part 2).
Pull of the solution The same test, done for you. One paste checks every section, marks the weak passages in place, and ranks what to fix first.
Anxiety of the new Two worries rather than one. Pasting confidential internal content into a website nobody has vetted, and getting back a number too vague to defend, since "the website said 62" will not win an argument about pausing publishing for a rewrite.
Habit of the present Style guides and manual review, reinforced by the most durable habit of all: "we'll fix the articles the agent gets wrong, once it gets one wrong."

Neither of those worries is a marketing problem. Each one settled a piece of the architecture, which is decisions 1 and 2 in Part 5.

The load-bearing observation: the two reasons a knowledge manager would refuse this tool are the two things it was architected around.

Part 2

The evidence

The manual test is being recommended in public, by the vendors selling the agents. Every quote below was re-checked at its own page in August 2026.

  • SearchUnify Building an AI-Ready Knowledge Base, published 1 July 2026

    "Pull a paragraph from the middle of each one, remove the title and surrounding context, and read it the way an agent would, as a standalone fragment. If that fragment alone isn't enough to produce a reliable answer, the knowledge base is the performance bottleneck."

    A later answer in the same guide gives the rule behind the test: "if a section can't be understood without reading what came before it, it needs to be rewritten." Read the guide.

  • Rankscale Chunkability for RAG, under the heading "The paste-into-a-blank-doc test"

    "Cut it, paste it into a blank document. Read it cold… Rewrite. Repeat for every paragraph on the page."

    A different vendor arriving at the same hands-on routine. Read the module.

  • Rezolve.ai on auditing the content you already have

    "It's common for only a small percentage of content to be AI-ready in its current form."

    Read the post.

  • RefineTrain AI Y Combinator, W22
    A funded company whose business is rewriting and optimising internal documentation for LLMs, which tells me this is a fundable problem rather than a private irritation.
  • Gartner press release, March 2025
    By 2029, agentic AI will autonomously resolve 80% of common customer service issues without human intervention, leading to a 30% reduction in operational costs. Gartner press release.

Freshness check, August 2026

This freshness check matters because "AI readiness checker" has become a crowded label, so I re-checked the space before publishing. Most new arrivals resemble Rank Prompt's free AI Readiness Checker: you give it a web address and it reports on crawler access, schema markup, sitemaps and llms.txt. The closest real overlap is NotionCue's free AI Readability Checker, which does take pasted text and does score answer-first structure and heading hierarchy. It is built to get a public page quoted in AI answers, though, and it does not test whether a section survives retrieval on its own. The gap is narrower than it was, and it holds.

Where this evidence is weak

The weakness is not accuracy, it is source type. Three of the five sources are vendor guides and the fourth is a company with something to sell. I have no first-hand complaint from working technical writers, which is why gathering it is item three on the roadmap, scheduled before launch messaging rather than after.

Part 3

Market and incentives

The market case rests on two outside numbers and no others. Gartner's projection above is the demand driver. For size, knowledge management software is projected to reach $75.22 billion by 2034 at a 12.3% annual growth rate (Straits Research), which is worth reading as an order of magnitude, because the same firm publishes a materially different forecast for the same market. I am not claiming a share of it, only locating the tool inside it.

The tool sits in the author's workflow, at the point where an article either becomes answerable or does not. That point is upstream of the retrieval software and the agent, and it is also what enterprise platforms sell as a paid readiness audit rather than as software you can use yourself.

Why the incumbents will not build this

What follows infers motive from business model, so I flag it as my reading rather than anything these companies have said.

  • Enterprise knowledge platforms such as Bloomfire, SearchUnify, Rezolve and DevRev sell AI readiness as audits, modules and services, and a free paste-an-article tool eats into that revenue. The pricing is the tell: Bloomfire publishes its own guide on auditing a knowledge base for AI readiness and sends you to a sales form rather than a self-serve plan.
  • AI search visibility tools make their money from marketers who want public pages quoted in AI answers. An internal knowledge-base author has neither a public page nor a budget line.
  • Retrieval infrastructure vendors sell to the developers who configure pipelines, not to the writers who produce what those pipelines read.

Any of the three could build this in a sprint, so the opening is not technical. It is a gap in incentives, which is a sturdier thing to sit behind, and the same shape as the argument in my previous teardown about why Google will not fix Takeout's metadata. A gap that exists because nobody thought of it closes the moment somebody does. A gap that exists because closing it costs the incumbent money stays open.

Both near misses are closer than a competitive table usually admits. Part 5

What I built

I built one web page. You paste in an article and get a score out of 100 for how well an agent could answer from it, across five checks: whether sections stand alone, whether the answer comes first, whether references point at something nearby, whether each section holds one idea, and whether the structure is clear. The checks are weighted 25/20/20/15/20 with 19 rules underneath, every finding is marked in your own text, and every point lost is itemised.

Marty Cagan's four product risks are the spine here: value (will anyone want it), usability (can they use it), feasibility (can it be built) and viability (does it work for the business). Each decision names the one it settles.

Decision 1

Fixed, explainable rules instead of an AI grader

Risk addressed: value, and trust

The obvious build sends the article to a language model and asks for a score, and I chose not to. Every point lost shows the sentence that caused it, why it matters to an agent, and what to write instead. A knowledge manager asking to pause publishing for a rewrite has to show a colleague the sentence and the reason, not a number from a model that might say something different tomorrow.

  • CostBlunter than judging meaning, and it will flag some good writing.
  • BoughtThe same article always scores the same, the result can be defended in a meeting, and there are no servers to run.
Decision 2

Everything runs in your browser, with no network requests

Risk addressed: viability, and confidentiality

Knowledge-base articles hold internal procedures, unreleased features, escalation paths and customer names in examples, so any tool that asks an author to paste one in has to answer "where does this go?" first. Here it goes nowhere, and you do not have to take my word for it: turn off your Wi-Fi and run it again. The page also ships a content security policy that refuses outbound connections of any kind, so the browser enforces the promise rather than my discipline. If a future dependency ever tried to phone home, it would be blocked rather than trusted.

  • CostNo usage data, now or ever.
  • BoughtAn author can try it on a real article without asking anyone's permission.
Decision 3

Keeping the formatting when you paste

Risk addressed: usability

Authors do not write in plain text markup. They copy from a finished page in Zendesk, Confluence or ServiceNow, and pasting that as plain text flattens the headings into ordinary sentences. Headings are where sections begin, the section is the unit every check scores, and four of the five checks have rules that read the headings themselves, so the tool converts the formatted version of what you copied instead.

  • CostOne conversion library and its edge cases, in a project that otherwise has almost none.
  • BoughtThe first paste works, and there is no second chance at a first paste.
Decision 4

A cap on how often one rule can cost you points

Risk addressed: value

Only the first three findings from any one rule count against the score, and the rest are listed as the same habit rather than fresh evidence. One bad habit repeated thirty times is one lesson, not a zero, and a rubric that returns a zero teaches the author only that the tool is hostile. This is the change that most affects whether a score gets shared with the team or quietly closed.

  • CostTwo genuinely poor articles can score closer together than they deserve.
  • BoughtScores that feel fair, and fairness is what makes a rubric adoptable.
Decision 5

The words authors use, not the words engineers use

Risk addressed: usability

The interface says "section" and never "chunk", and the ideas the rubric rests on are explained rather than assumed. This sounds cosmetic, but it is the positioning made concrete: the category is full of tools that speak to people who already know the jargon and empty of tools that speak to the person writing the words.

  • CostLess appeal to the developer crowd, who are also the crowd most likely to upvote a launch post.
  • BoughtThe audience nobody else is serving.
Decision 6

Saying what already works, not just what is broken

Risk addressed: value, as a Kano delighter

The report also picks out what is working, such as headings written as questions and numbered lists for procedures. None of it earns points, because it is recognition rather than scoring. A tool that only subtracts teaches authors to avoid it, and an author who runs this once has not changed how they write, whereas one who runs it twice has.

  • CostScreen space, and part of the report's attention budget.
  • BoughtIt reads as a coach rather than a critic, which decides whether it gets opened again.
Decision 7

Every rule tested twice, including a near miss

Risk addressed: feasibility

Every rule ships with an example it must catch and a near miss it must leave alone. "This setting controls the retry interval" is not a vague pronoun even though it opens with "This", and a rule that cannot tell the two apart will flag good writing and lose the author in one sitting. The near miss is the test that matters, because a rule-based checker rarely fails by missing problems and usually fails by crying wolf.

  • CostSlower rule writing, since a rule is not done until both tests exist.
  • BoughtRubric integrity. In this product the rubric is the product.
Decision 8

A cut list, written down before the build

Risk addressed: viability, and focus

No accounts, no server, no integrations, no batch mode, no automatic rewriting and no file upload, all written down before the first line of code so that later enthusiasm could not reopen the argument. Each of those is defensible alone. Together they are a different product with a server, a privacy caveat and no shipping date.

  • CostEvery power user will ask for one of them, and some will leave.
  • BoughtA tool that shipped, and a promise with no small print.

Kano classification

The Kano model sorts features by how people react to them. Reading headings correctly is the must-have, because getting it wrong puts every section boundary in the wrong place, and nobody reports that as a parsing bug because they simply stop believing the score. Marking passages in place and scoring each check separately are performance features, where more precision is straightforwardly better. The delighters are the line on every finding explaining why it matters to an agent, and a privacy claim you can test by switching off your network.

Business viability, stated plainly

The business case is that there is no revenue model and none is planned. Willingness to pay exists, since enterprise platforms charge for this analysis as audits, but free with no strings is the position rather than a plan to charge later. What I am betting on is the judgement on display, in the public version of the work I do, which is organising knowledge content so autonomous agents can resolve tickets.

What I got wrong, and what is still open

  1. The weights are judgement, not evidence. The 25/20/20/15/20 split is my ranking of what damages an answer most, with no dataset of failed articles behind it. Defensible, not validated.
  2. Fixed rules misfire on good writing. The cap and the near-miss tests limit the damage, but the score is a strong hint rather than a verdict until it is calibrated against real production articles.
  3. The demand evidence is vendor-sourced. Strong on the existence of the manual test, thin on what it feels like to do it.
  4. The rules only work properly in English. The tool warns when the text does not look like English, and a warning is not a fix.
  5. I ran this page through the tool, and it marked me down. Answer first was the weakest check, because several sections opened with context instead of the point. Those openings are rewritten, which is the rubric doing its job on its author. It also flagged the Cost and Bought lines in every decision as buried steps, and I left those alone, because they are a summary rather than a procedure. That is exactly the false positive risk 2 describes, found in my own writing.
  6. The rubric can never be tuned from usage. No funnel, no drop-off, no spread of real scores to calibrate the bands against. That is the price of decision 2, paid knowingly, because the alternative breaks the claim the tool rests on.

What I would do next

RICE scores reach times impact times confidence, divided by effort, and it suits this situation because reach punishes feature work on a tool nobody can find yet. Reach is a relative score out of 10 rather than a user count, since I have no traffic data. Effort is in person-days, and both effort and impact are my estimates.

Part 7

What I would cut

The Blue Ocean ERRC grid asks what to eliminate, reduce, raise and create against the norms of the category. It suits a cut list, because the plan is to compete where competitors are not looking rather than to out-feature them where they are.

Eliminate

  • Servers and uploads.
  • Accounts.
  • Usage tracking.
  • Integrations.
  • Automatic rewriting.

Each breaks either the privacy promise or the focus.

Reduce

  • Scope, to one pasted article at a time.
  • Settings, to none. A rubric you can adjust is a rubric you can talk out of a bad score.

Raise

  • Explanation, so every point lost is accounted for.
  • Trust, through a claim you can test offline.
  • Plain language, so the ideas behind the score reach people who do not build software.

Create

  • A free, zero-install version of what the enterprise layer sells as consulting.

Deliberately left out

No personas, because there is one well-understood user here and inventing three archetypes of a person I already understand is box-ticking. No North Star metric or HEART, because both need usage data that decision 2 rules out, and a metric I cannot measure only invites false confidence. No OKRs, because this is a portfolio piece rather than an organisation.
