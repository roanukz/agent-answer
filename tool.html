How scoring works

An AI support agent does not read your article the way a person does. Before the agent sees your writing, software splits the article into pieces, usually one piece per section, and stores each piece on its own. (Engineers call these pieces chunks. Everywhere else, this tool just says sections.) When someone asks the agent a question, that software fetches the few sections that best match the question, and the agent answers from those sections alone, without the title, the intro, or whatever came before.

The usual way to test for this is by hand: pull a paragraph out of the article, read it cold with no context around it, rewrite it if it cannot stand on its own, then repeat for every paragraph. This tool runs that test for you, using fixed rules that explain themselves. The same article always gets the same score. No AI model is involved in the scoring, and nothing you paste leaves your browser.

The score

Each of the five checks starts at 100 points. Every major finding costs 25 points and every minor finding costs 10. Info findings cost nothing, because they are worth knowing without being worth punishing. Only the first three findings from any one rule count against the score. Anything past that is still listed, tagged "not double-counted, same habit", because ten instances of one habit is one lesson rather than ten. A check never drops below 0.

The overall score is a weighted average of the five checks:

Self-contained sections25%
Answer first (BLUF)20%

85 and up: agent-ready. 60 to 84: needs edits before your agent can rely on it. Below 60: an agent will struggle to answer from this. The same bands apply to each individual check, as pass, needs work, or fail.

Check 1: Self-contained sections (25%)

Agents retrieve individual sections, not whole articles. A section that only makes sense after reading what came before it will be retrieved alone, and misread alone. The principle is chunk self-containment: every section has to work by itself.

  • Orphan opener (major, costs 25). The section's first sentence starts with a bare pronoun ("It", "This", "They") or a connective ("However", "Therefore", "Additionally"). Retrieved alone, the section opens mid-thought. The agent has no idea what "this" refers to, and a connective promises a contrast with text the agent never saw.
  • Cross-section pointer (major, costs 25). Phrases like "as mentioned above", "see below", or "in the previous section". The agent sees one section at a time, so "above" does not exist for it.
  • Generic heading (minor, costs 10). Headings like "Overview", "Notes", "More information", or a bare "Step 3". The heading travels with its section as the label retrieval matches against, and a generic label matches nothing in particular.
  • Orphan acronym (minor, costs 10). An acronym is spelled out once, as in "Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)", then used bare in a different section. The section holding the expansion may not be retrieved alongside the one using the acronym.

Check 2: Answer first, or BLUF (20%)

Agents, and the software that picks which sections to retrieve, weigh the start of a section heavily. Burying the answer under context makes a section both harder to find and harder to answer from. The principle is bottom line up front.

  • Preamble opener (major, costs 25). Throat-clearing first sentences such as "In this article", "Before you begin", or "It is important to note". The first sentence is the most valuable one you have, so spend it on the answer rather than on announcing the article.
  • Buried steps (minor, costs 10). Three or more sentences of prose before the section's steps. An agent asked "how do I" should reach the steps immediately.
  • Slow start (minor, costs 10). A first paragraph over 60 words that neither gives an instruction nor restates the topic named in the heading. If the opening does not restate the topic, retrieval has weaker evidence that this section answers the question.

Check 3: Unresolved references (20%)

Pronouns and pointers that rely on distant context break when text is split into sections. The agent cannot work out what they refer to, and may answer about the wrong thing. The principle is entity resolution: every "it" needs its noun nearby.

  • Dangling pointer (major, costs 25). "The following", "the above", or "the previous", with no list or table in the same section to point at. A "the following" sentence that introduces the list right underneath is fine, because the target travels with the section.
  • Bare demonstrative (minor, costs 10). A paragraph that opens "This causes" or "Those require". Any paragraph may be quoted alone in an answer, and an opener like "This" forces the agent to guess what it means. "This setting controls" is fine, because the noun is right there.
  • Ambiguous "it" (minor, costs 10). A paragraph that opens "It takes" or "It fails". Formal patterns such as "It is recommended" are not flagged.
  • Relative time (info, costs nothing). "Currently", "recently", "soon", "the latest release". Agents cannot tell when the article was written, so "currently" goes wrong silently. No points are deducted, but a date or a version number is worth more.

Check 4: One idea per section (15%)

Retrieval matches a question to a section. A section covering three ideas matches all three questions weakly instead of matching one strongly. The principle is one idea per chunk.

  • Section too long (minor, costs 10 over 300 words; major, costs 25 over 500). Oversized sections get split wherever the software decides, mid-sentence and mid-idea, instead of where you would have split them.
  • Topic shift (minor, costs 10). Two or more paragraphs in one section starting with "Additionally", "You can also", or "Alternatively". Each extra idea dilutes the section's match to any one question.
  • No headings (major, costs 25). A document over 200 words with no headings at all. Without headings, the splitting is done by length instead of by you.

Check 5: Structure signals (20%)

Headings, lists and tables are structure an agent can read reliably. Walls of prose are not. The principle is structure parseability: make the shape of the article visible.

  • Low heading density (minor, costs 10). More than 400 words per heading on average. Fewer headings means bigger retrieval units and vaguer matches.
  • Heading jump (info, costs nothing). A heading level that skips down more than one step, such as # straight to ###. Skipped levels scramble the section tree that some software relies on.
  • Comparison in prose (minor, costs 10). Two or more "whereas", "compared to" or "in contrast" sentences with no table in the section. Agents answer comparison questions far more reliably from a table.
  • Sequence in prose (minor, costs 10). "First, then, next, finally" packed into one paragraph with no numbered list in the section. Steps hidden in a paragraph get paraphrased and reordered. A numbered list gets quoted.
  • Wall of text (minor, costs 10). Any single paragraph over 120 words. Long paragraphs get split blindly, mid-thought.

Strengths

The report also points out what is already agent-ready. There are no points for it, just recognition: headings written as questions or tasks ("How do I reset my token", "Reset your token"), tables for comparisons, and numbered steps. If your article shows strengths, keep writing like that.

Honest limitations

Every rule here is a fixed rule of thumb, tuned for English-language KB writing, with no machine learning and no calls to any service. Rules of thumb have edge cases. When a finding is wrong for your article you can see exactly why it fired, judge it yourself, and ignore it with a clear conscience. A score that cannot explain itself is a toy, and every point lost on this page can explain itself.

One consequence of the weighting is worth knowing. The three checks about meaning, which are self-containment, answer first and references, carry 65% of the score, and pure structure carries 35%. An article whose only problem is structural, one giant unbroken section for example, can still land in the 80s. So read the five check scores rather than just the headline number. A failing check is a failing check, whatever the total says.
